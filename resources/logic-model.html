Free one-pager · Program development

Start with the change.
Work back to the plan.

A logic model template is one page that shows how a program is supposed to work: what you put in, what you do, what you can count, and what you expect to change. This free template is built for small nonprofits, small businesses, and program teams. It takes about forty-five minutes, alone or with one colleague, and the second page shows a filled-in example.

What is on the page

Ten boxes,
filled in by number.

Funders often ask for a logic model because it shows the reasoning behind a program: if these resources are in place, these activities can happen, and if they happen, these changes should follow. Filling in the change first keeps the activities tied to what they are for.

  1. 01

    The situation.

    Who has the problem, what it is, and why now. One or two sentences.

  2. 02

    The bigger goal.

    The change in the community or organization your work contributes to. Funders call this impact.

  3. 03

    Longer-term change.

    What people will do differently, or how their situation changes, a year or more from now.

  4. 04

    Short-term change.

    What people will know, be able to do, or believe within months.

  5. 05

    What you do.

    The main activities, in plain verbs.

  6. 06

    What you can count.

    The outputs that show the work happened: sessions held, people reached, materials delivered.

  7. 07

    What you put in.

    The people, partners, time, money, space, and materials it takes. Funders call these inputs.

  8. 08

    What you are assuming.

    What has to be true for the chain to hold.

  9. 09

    What is outside your control.

    The conditions that could help or get in the way.

  10. 10

    How you will know.

    One or two measures for the changes you most need to show: what, who collects it, and when.

A filled-in example

Fix-It Saturdays,
a made-up program.

This example is invented for illustration: a neighborhood hardware store with eight staff that wants fewer returned parts and more customers who come back. It shows how the boxes connect, names no client, and reports no results.

01 The situation
Many customers buy parts for simple home repairs, then return them unused because they were unsure how to do the job. Staff answer the same questions at the counter every weekend, and the store has open floor space on Saturday mornings.
02 The bigger goal
Neighbors handle more small repairs themselves, and the store is where they come for help.
03 Longer-term change
Customers who attend come back for their next project. Fewer parts come back unused. Some regulars bring a neighbor.
04 Short-term change
Attendees can do one common repair on their own, such as a leaky faucet, a wall patch, or a running toilet, and know which part and tool to buy before they start.
05 What we do
A monthly one-hour Saturday class on one repair, hands-on with practice materials. A one-page how-to card for each repair. A question table at the counter after class.
06 What we can count
Classes held. Attendees, from the sign-in sheet. How-to cards handed out. Questions answered at the table.
07 What we put in
Two staff who teach, two hours per class including setup. The owner's time to plan the schedule. Floor space and a practice bench. Practice parts from a supplier. A small budget for printed cards and coffee.
08 What we are assuming
Customers want to do small repairs themselves. Saturday morning works for them. Staff are comfortable teaching a small group.
09 Outside our control
Larger stores run their own classes. Weather and seasonal projects change turnout. Supplier donations may vary.
10 How we will know
The sign-in count each month (staff). A two-question check at the end of class: "Could you do this on your own now? What will you try next?" (staff). Unused-part returns each month, compared with the months before the classes, from the register (owner).

Who it is for

Nonprofit directors, program leads, owners, and boards with a program to explain.

Use it to plan a new program or service before you build it, to rewrite a program description for a grant, or to give your board or team one page that shows how the work leads to results. When you are ready to test the smallest version first, pair it with the Pilot Plan.

Sources and further reading

  • W.K. Kellogg Foundation, Logic Model Development Guide, chapter 2: Developing a Basic Logic Model for Your Program (2004). The source of the advice to fill in outcomes first.
  • Cooperative Extension, Logic Models: A Tool for Program Planning and Assessment, drawing on the University of Wisconsin Extension model. The source of the situation, assumptions, and outside factors boxes.
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Program Evaluation Framework Action Guide, Step 2: Describe the Program. The source of the order of outcomes, from short-term to longer-term.
