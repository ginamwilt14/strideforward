The coaching conversation starts before the coach walks in.

On pre-conference protocols, unspoken agreements, and how coaches earn the right to be in a classroom.

By Dr. Gina Wilt · September 8, 2026

Most instructional coaching goes wrong in a five-minute window that happens before anyone has said a word.

It's the walk from the front office to the classroom.

In that walk, the coach is thinking about the observation form, the last time they visited this teacher, the leadership priority for the month, and whether they'll be back in time for the meeting at eleven. The teacher, meanwhile, is watching a stranger in professional clothes walk toward their door and is running the entire relationship in their head — what does this person want from me, what will they report, what does this cost me if I get it wrong.

By the time the coach sits down in the back of the room, both people are already inside a conversation neither of them has agreed to.

That is the conversation that shapes what the observation does. Not the debrief. Not the feedback protocol. The unspoken one, which was decided before the coach walked in.

The unspoken agreement problem

Coaching relationships are governed less by the stated protocols than by the unspoken agreements both parties bring into the room. What the coach thinks the observation is for. What the teacher thinks it will be used for. Whether it is developmental or evaluative. Whether the coach is a resource or a signal from leadership.

None of that is on the observation form. All of it is decided by lunch.

The trap for coaches is assuming the stated protocol handles the agreement. It doesn't. A coach can walk in with the cleanest formative-not-evaluative framing and still be received as a threat, because the teacher's read of the agreement is shaped by everything that came before this moment — every prior coach, every prior evaluation cycle, every leadership rotation in the building.

The unspoken agreement, in other words, is not something the coach can declare into existence. It is something the coach has to build. And it does not get built in the observation. It gets built in the pre-conference.

The pre-conference protocol

Here is the shape of a pre-conference that earns the right to be in the room. It is not long. Twenty minutes, done well, does more than an hour done casually.

Open with the teacher's question, not yours.

"What is happening in your classroom right now that you want a second set of eyes on?"

That question does something a coach cannot fake. It puts the teacher in the driver's seat of the observation. It tells the teacher that the coach is not there to collect evidence against a leadership rubric. It tells the teacher that this observation is for them.

If the teacher says "nothing, I'm fine, whatever you need to see," the pre-conference stops there. You do not have an agreement. You are welcome to observe, but the coaching hasn't started yet. That is important information.

Name what the observation is for — and what it is not.

Out loud. Explicit. "This will not be shared with your evaluator. This is not part of your formal evaluation cycle. What I write down is yours."

If any part of that isn't true — if you do report to the principal, if the notes do go somewhere — say that too. Coaches who fudge this line lose teachers permanently the first time it comes out sideways. Coaches who name it clearly, even when the answer is uncomfortable, are the ones teachers eventually trust.

Agree on the focus, in writing, in one sentence.

Not a rubric. A sentence. "During today's observation, I'm going to watch specifically for how students are responding when you release them to independent work — because that's the transition you said felt shaky."

The sentence has to be the teacher's, edited by the coach. Not the coach's, offered to the teacher for approval. If the coach writes the sentence and the teacher agrees, you have input. If the teacher writes the sentence and the coach helps sharpen it, you have voice — and a coaching relationship that will still be there in April.

Watch for

A pre-conference that ends without a one-sentence focus in the teacher's own words is a pre-conference that hasn't happened yet. Slow down and try again.

What this earns you

Coaches who run a real pre-conference get three things they otherwise have to work for the entire year.

They get a teacher who is ready to be observed. They get a focus that the teacher owns. And they get permission — not administrative permission, teacher permission — to say hard things in the debrief.

That third one is the whole game. Coaching that stays surface is coaching without permission. Coaching that moves practice is coaching a teacher has invited into the difficult part of their week.

You cannot ask for that permission after the observation. You have to earn it before you walk in the room.

The five-minute walk from the office to the classroom is where coaching either succeeds or quietly doesn't. What determines which one it is happened before the walk started.

That is the part of coaching most protocols leave out. It is also the part that decides everything else.
