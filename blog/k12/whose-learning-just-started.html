When your PL session ends, whose learning just started?

On designing so the teacher leaves owning the next move, instead of the facilitator.

By Dr. Gina Wilt · September 8, 2026

There is a moment near the end of every good PL session that almost everyone misses.

It's the twenty minutes after the last slide.

Not the exit ticket. Not the feedback form. The twenty minutes where the teacher who is going to try the thing on Monday is quietly rehearsing it in their head, deciding what they will change, and (most importantly) deciding what they will let go of to make room for it.

That is the moment the learning starts.

Most sessions are designed for the facilitator's clock, not the teacher's. The clock says: content, discussion, activity, reflection, close. The teacher's clock says: okay, but which of the four things I already do is the one I'm going to stop doing to try this?

If your session doesn't answer that question, the learning ends when the session does.

The question that changes everything

Here is the single most useful move I know for PL design. It's a question, and it goes at the end of the session, in place of most of what usually goes there.

"What are you going to stop doing to make room for what you just learned?"

That question does more work than every reflection prompt I've seen in a decade. Here's why.

Teachers do not have empty space in their week. They have a full week. Any new practice you teach them displaces something. If the session doesn't name the displacement, the teacher does, silently, in the car on the way home, and the thing that gets displaced is whatever the teacher was already less sure about. Which may or may not be the thing you wanted.

Making the trade explicit does three things at once. It surfaces which existing practice the teacher has been quietly questioning. It creates a real moment about capacity. And it moves the teacher from learning about the new practice to owning the substitution, which is the only version of learning that survives contact with a Tuesday morning.

Design implications

If you take the stop-doing question seriously, the whole shape of a PL session changes.

You spend less time on the front end selling the why (teachers already know their classrooms are hard) and more time on the back end helping them design the swap. You bring the tools for the new practice, and you also bring a slot in the agenda for teachers to write down what they are letting go.

You stop treating the end of the session as the end of the work. You treat it as the beginning of the next week.

Practically, this looks like:

  • A one-page planner (not a reflection form) that has two columns: what I'm going to try, and what I'm going to stop.
  • A named handoff: the teacher writes down one person they'll tell about the swap, and when.
  • A follow-up cadence: not "let us know how it went," but a specific check-in point two weeks out, with a specific question: "Did the swap hold?"

None of this is expensive. All of it takes design intention that most sessions do not bring, because most sessions are designed for the room, not the Tuesday.

The facilitator's discipline

There's a discipline behind this move, and it is a hard one for facilitators, myself included.

The discipline is: your session is not the point.

The point is what the teacher does on Monday. Everything about your design (the pacing, the examples, the tools, the closing) should be organized around that. Which means, sometimes, cutting content you love because it doesn't survive the substitution test. If the teacher can't tell you what they're going to stop doing to try it, the content is not landing. It's stacking.

The best PL facilitators I've worked with all share this trait: they finish the session with less content on the wall than they started with, because they made room. They made room in the session for teachers to design the swap. And when they left the room, the learning was already underway.

That is the version of professional learning that shifts classroom practice.

Not because it was better content. Because it left the teacher with a decision they had already made.

You made it to the end. That is more than most to-do lists get.
