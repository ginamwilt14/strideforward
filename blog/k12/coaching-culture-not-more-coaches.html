You don't need more coaches. You need a coaching culture.

Five moves any building leader can make Monday — none of them require a budget line.

By Dr. Gina Wilt · September 8, 2026

The most common request I hear from K-12 educators and leaders right now is some version of the same sentence:

"We need more coaches, but we can't afford them."

The sentence is true. It is also the wrong problem.

Adding coaches to a building that does not have a coaching culture is like adding a filter to a pond that has no circulation. It cleans a small section, briefly. The rest of the water is unchanged, and the filter gets clogged.

What most schools need is not more coaching positions. It is more people in the building who can coach — who can hold a hard conversation, ask a real question, and stay in it. That is a culture problem, and culture problems are not solved by hiring.

They are solved by design.

What a coaching culture really is

A coaching culture is a building where the skill of coaching is distributed across roles, not concentrated in a job title.

It is a place where a grade-level chair can coach a colleague through a hard parent conversation. Where a department head can watch a lesson and give feedback that changes what happens tomorrow. Where the assistant principal, on a walkthrough, can name what they saw in a way that makes the teacher want to try something.

None of those people are formally coaches. All of them are doing coaching work. The culture is the reason they can.

The distinction matters because the alternative — waiting for a dedicated coach to reach every teacher — is arithmetic that does not work. A typical instructional coach in a K-12 setting can meaningfully partner with maybe eight to twelve teachers over a year. A building with sixty teachers cannot solve its instructional support problem by hiring. It has to solve it by distributing the practice.

That is the shift most leaders miss. They think of coaching as a role. It is a skillset. And skillsets can be built.

Five moves any building leader can make Monday

None of these require a budget line. All of them require intention.

1. Name the coaching moves that already happen — and give them credit.

Every building already has coaching happening. A veteran teacher coaching a first-year one in the copy room. A grade-level lead coaching a colleague through a parent meeting. It is invisible because nobody calls it that.

Start calling it that. When you see it happening, name it. "That was coaching. That was a real question. That was a hard conversation held well." Naming builds a shared vocabulary, and shared vocabulary is where a culture starts.

2. Add coaching to the definition of a distinguished teacher.

Most evaluation rubrics reward classroom performance and student outcomes. Very few reward the ability to develop other teachers. If your rubric doesn't value coaching, your best teachers won't invest in it — they'll invest in what is measured.

Add it. Not as a punishment for the ones who don't do it, but as a recognition for the ones who do. The change is small and the signal is enormous.

3. Teach two coaching moves — and require them in team meetings.

Not a full coaching curriculum. Two moves. My recommendations are:

  • The paraphrase. "So what I'm hearing is..." said out loud before offering advice. It slows the conversation and it doubles the chance the advice will land.
  • The open question. "What have you tried?" instead of "Have you tried X?" One is coaching. The other is telling.

Introduce both in a PL session. Then require them in your grade-level meetings for a month. Watch what happens.

4. Protect the pre-conference for every observation, from anyone.

If the culture treats observation as a coach's job, only the coach's observations feel developmental. If the culture treats observation as everyone's job — with the same protocols — every observation becomes a coaching opportunity.

The most important protocol to protect is the pre-conference. Any observer, formal coach or not, sits down with the teacher for fifteen minutes first and asks the question the teacher wants a second set of eyes on. That single move turns observation from surveillance into coaching, regardless of who is doing it.

5. Model the moves as the leader.

The last one is the hardest, and it is the one that decides everything else.

If you, as the building leader, do not coach your assistant principals, they will not coach the department heads. If the department heads don't coach the grade leads, the grade leads won't coach the teachers.

Culture is what the leader does, watched. Not what the leader says.

If you want a coaching culture, be coached — visibly. Ask an assistant principal to observe you in a hard meeting and debrief you. Let your team see you receive a hard question and stay in it. That is the single most cultural move a leader can make, and it does not cost anything except the discomfort of being seen.

That discomfort is the price of the culture you want.

Try this week

Pick one of the five moves. Not all five. Run it for a full week and watch what shifts. Culture is built one visible move at a time.

The math that changes

The buildings that make these five moves do not have more coaches. They have more coaching. The difference shows up in fidelity, in teacher retention, and in the quiet metric nobody tracks — how often a teacher walks into a colleague's classroom because they wanted to, not because the schedule made them.

That is the real measure of a coaching culture.

It is also the one nobody can hire their way to.
