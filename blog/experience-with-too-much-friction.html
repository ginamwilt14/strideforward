The experience with too much friction.

The customers, families, and donors who leave rarely tell you why. The friction usually shows up long before the exit, and much of it can be fixed once you know where to look.

By Gina Wilt, EdD, MBA · September 3, 2026 · Updated October 17, 2026

In short

  • Customers, families, and donors who leave rarely name a reason. What pushed them out was usually friction that added up over several small moments.
  • Friction tends to hide in three places: the handoff, the follow-through, and the recovery when something goes wrong.
  • In the next 30 days, map one path a customer, family, or donor takes, give the moment nobody owns to one person, and design one moment people will remember.

The people who stop showing up almost never write you a letter. A customer who cancels doesn't usually cite the third bad interaction. They cite something abstract, if they cite anything at all. A family who leaves a school rarely names the specific enrollment call that soured. A donor who lapses doesn't send a note explaining that the receipt email arrived six weeks late. What each of them experienced was friction, accumulated. What they told you, if they told you anything, was a shrug.

This is the hardest thing about customer experience work. The signal is diffuse. The people who had a great experience are often the ones who fill out the surveys. The people who had a friction-filled experience mostly go silent. So the picture the organization has of itself (from surveys, from testimonials, from the customer stories the team repeats at all-hands meetings) tends to be flattering. For a meaningful share of the people you serve, the experience has more friction in it than anyone inside the organization realizes.

Where friction usually hides

Friction rarely lives in the moments the organization spends the most time on. Those moments (the pitch, the enrollment day, the gala, the onboarding week) get rehearsed. The team knows how to run them. Friction builds up in the moments between: the ones nobody owns.

The handoff.

When responsibility passes from one team, one person, or one system to another. Sales to delivery. Enrollment to the first-week teacher. Development to program. The customer feels the seam. They have to repeat information they already gave. They wait longer than expected. They lose confidence that anyone knows their situation. Nothing is technically broken, and the customer still feels that something is off.

The follow-through.

The second interaction after the first big one. The check-in email that never comes. The promised call-back that lands three days late. The receipt that shows up eventually but without the personal touch the donor expected. The onboarding week that was great, followed by a week of silence. This is often the friction that decides whether someone stays or leaves, because it's the moment they find out whether the organization's care was a peak or a pattern.

The recovery.

What happens when something goes wrong. A missed deadline. A form that didn't submit. A frustrated call. Almost every customer, family, or donor has a recovery moment somewhere in their experience. The organizations that keep people treat recovery as its own designed moment, with a human, an apology, and a clear next step. The organizations that lose people let recovery get bounced between departments until the person gives up.

Quick diagnostic

Ask three of your team members to trace a customer, family, or donor from first contact to their most recent interaction. Have them mark every handoff, every follow-through gap, and every recovery moment. Compare the maps. The disagreements are where the friction lives.

Three settings, one pattern

The vocabulary changes, and the pattern stays much the same across the organizations I work with.

In a small business, the friction usually lives in the space between marketing and delivery. The pitch was clear; the first invoice was confusing. The onboarding call was warm; the second week was silent. The service was strong; the renewal conversation happened three months late and felt transactional. When the customers who leave explain at all, they describe a cumulative feeling that the promise wasn't quite kept, even when on paper it was.

In a nonprofit, the friction usually lives in the donor and beneficiary experience between the big moments. The gala was beautiful; the thank-you letter came eight weeks later on a template. The beneficiary intake was warm; the follow-up service required them to retell their whole story to a different case worker. The volunteer orientation was moving; the second shift had no one assigned to greet the newcomer at the door. Each of these small moments is fixable. Together they build the story a donor or beneficiary tells themselves about what the organization is like.

In a school or education organization, the friction usually lives in the family experience during transitions. Enrollment was warm; the first week required them to fill out three forms they had already completed. The teacher meeting was thoughtful; the follow-up email chain got lost in three inboxes. The program launch was engaging; the mid-year check-in never happened. Families who leave tend to describe a general feeling of being one more file in the pile.

What to do in the next 30 days

A common mistake is treating customer experience as a big project: a big audit, a big report, a big set of recommendations. That work has its place, and it is rarely the fastest way to reduce friction. Three moves that work in a month:

Pick one path and map it.

Start with one path: a new customer, a first-time family, or a new donor. Trace it end to end with the people who touch it every day. Use a whiteboard, a shared doc, or the fillable one-pager from our Customer Experience Blueprint Starter Kit. The goal for now is to see that path clearly.

Find the invisible seam.

Once you have the map, look for the handoff, follow-through gap, or recovery moment that nobody currently owns. There is usually at least one. Give it to a specific person. Their job for the next 30 days is to reduce the friction in that moment, the one your organization is currently leaving to whoever picks up the phone next.

Design one moment that matters.

Research on how people remember experiences fits here. In studies led by Daniel Kahneman, people judged a past experience mostly by its most intense moment and by how it ended, a pattern known as the peak-end rule. Chip and Dan Heath turn that idea into practical advice in The Power of Moments. For your organization, it means you have real influence over how you will be remembered, if you're willing to design one moment. A follow-up call from a specific person. A handwritten note at a specific milestone. A recovery gesture that does more than the situation required. Design it once, then run it every time.

The organizations that hold onto their people notice friction early, and they design one moment that outweighs it.

Why this matters

Many organizations I've worked with underestimate how much of their growth depends on keeping the people they already serve. Every person who stays is one you do not have to find again, and a person who stays for years and tells others about you is hard to replace with any marketing. That's true whether the "customer" pays a fee, writes a check, or enrolls a child. Reducing friction in the experience is often the most useful work an organization is not doing yet.

The good news is that friction is one of the more workable problems on a leader's desk. Most of the time it can be done with the plan, budget, and people you already have. It takes an hour to see the experience clearly, a decision about who owns the seam, and the discipline to design one moment that earns the trust the rest of the experience is losing.

Apply it

Try this in the next 30 days

  • Pick one path: a new customer, a first-time family, or a new donor.
  • Trace it end to end with the people who touch it every day.
  • Find the handoff, follow-through gap, or recovery moment that nobody owns.
  • Give that moment to one person for the next 30 days.
  • Design one moment people will remember, such as a follow-up call from a specific person.

Common questions

What is friction in customer experience?

Friction is anything that makes a customer, family, or donor work harder than they expected: repeating information, waiting on a reply, chasing a form, or getting passed between people. One moment rarely drives someone away. The moments add up.

Why do customers and donors leave without saying why?

Most people do not trace their decision back to a single moment. They leave with a general feeling that the promise was not quite kept, so exit surveys and cancellation notes tend to name something vague, if they name anything.

Where does friction usually hide in a small business or nonprofit?

In the moments between the big ones: the handoff from one person or team to another, the follow-through after a first interaction, and the recovery when something goes wrong. Those moments often have no clear owner.

How do I find the friction in my customer or donor experience?

Pick one path people take through your organization, such as a new customer, a first-time family, or a new donor, and trace it end to end with the people who touch it every day. Mark every handoff, follow-through gap, and recovery moment, then give the one nobody owns to a specific person for 30 days.

What is the peak-end rule?

The peak-end rule describes a finding from research led by Daniel Kahneman: people tend to judge a past experience by its most intense moment and by how it ended, more than by its length or the average of every moment. Chip and Dan Heath build on it in The Power of Moments.

Sources and further reading

  • Daniel Kahneman, Barbara L. Fredrickson, Charles A. Schreiber, and Donald A. Redelmeier, “When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End,” Psychological Science 4, no. 6 (1993)
  • Chip Heath and Dan Heath, The Power of Moments: Why Certain Experiences Have Extraordinary Impact (Simon & Schuster, 2017)
