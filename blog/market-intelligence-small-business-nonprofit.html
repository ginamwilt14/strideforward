Know Who You Serve Before You Spend: Market Intelligence for Small Businesses and Nonprofits

Big decisions made on hunches are expensive. A focused look at your customers and your market can show you where to spend, where to partner, and what to stop.

Most small business owners and nonprofit leaders know their customers well, one at a time. You know the regulars by name. You know which families return every year. What is harder to see, from inside the day-to-day, is the pattern across all of them, and how your work compares with the other options they have.

That gap matters most right before a big decision: launching a new offer, raising prices, adding a location, applying for a grant, or pitching a partner. Those are the moments when a hunch can cost the most.

What market intelligence means for a small organization

Market intelligence sounds like something large companies buy by the report. For a small business or nonprofit, it is simpler: a clear, current picture of who you serve, what they are trying to get done, who else serves them, and what that means for your next move.

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) frames market research as a way to find customers for your business and to understand how other businesses in your market compete. That applies to nonprofits too. Your “market” includes the other programs, agencies, and options your participants and funders could choose.

Your buyer, user, and funder may be different people

This is one of the most common blind spots I see. A single service can have several customers, each with a different definition of success.

A tutoring business may be paid by parents, used by students, and referred by teachers. A nonprofit job program may be funded by a foundation, used by participants, and judged by employers who hire them. A marketing agency may be hired by an owner, used by a front-desk team, and measured by customers who never see the agency at all.

When those groups want different things, messaging and offers that land with one can miss the others. Naming each customer separately is the first step to serving all of them well.

Ask about progress, not features

Clayton Christensen and his colleagues argued in Harvard Business Review that customers do not simply buy products. They “hire” them to make progress in a specific situation. Their approach, often called jobs to be done, shifts the question from “What features do customers want?” to “What are they trying to get done, and what gets in the way?”

For a small organization, that shift is practical. A donor may be “hiring” your nonprofit to feel connected to a cause in their neighborhood. A small business client may be hiring your service to stop worrying about one part of their week. Once you know the job, you can see what to emphasize and what to drop.

What a landscape review shows you

A landscape review is a structured look at the other organizations and options in your space. It is not a list of rivals to worry about. It is a map of where you fit.

In September 2026, I completed a landscape review of 25 organizations serving young entrepreneurs, 15 national and 10 based in Arizona. The value was not the list itself. It was what the list made visible: which needs were well served, which were thinly served, and where a partnership would reach people faster than building something new.

A useful landscape review usually answers four questions:

  • Who else serves the same people? Include indirect options, like free resources or doing nothing.
  • What do they emphasize? Price, speed, convenience, quality, relationships, or mission.
  • Where are the gaps? Needs that no one serves well, or groups that are overlooked.
  • Who could be a partner? Organizations that serve the same people at a different stage.

Use public data before you commission anything

You do not need a large budget to start. Census Business Builder, a free U.S. Census Bureau tool, combines demographic and economic data to help owners understand a market before opening or expanding. The Census Bureau’s small business data tools page lists others. The SBA also connects owners with free counseling through Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs) and SCORE mentors.

Public data tells you about the size and shape of a market. Conversations tell you why people choose what they choose. You need both.

Turn findings into a decision

Research is only useful if it ends in a decision. When StrideForward does market intelligence work with a client, the deliverable is built around the choice in front of them:

  • A market snapshot: who you serve, what they need, and the public data behind it, on a few pages you can use.
  • A landscape review: the other options your customers consider, with gaps and possible partners marked.
  • Customer conversations: short interviews with buyers, users, or funders, summarized as patterns.
  • A decision brief: what the findings support for your offer, pricing, positioning, or partnerships, and what they do not.

I have used this approach to help complex organizations make their offerings clear to outside buyers. The principle scales down well: when you know who you serve and what they are choosing between, the next decision gets easier to make and easier to explain.

Apply it

A one-hour market check

  • List your buyer, user, and funder or referrer. Note where they are the same person.
  • For each, write the result they are trying to get, in their words.
  • Name five other organizations or options they could choose instead of you, including doing nothing.
  • Pull one public data point about your market from Census Business Builder.
  • Write one decision this information supports, and one question it leaves open.

Common questions

How can a small business or nonprofit do market research?

Start by naming your buyer, user, and funder or referrer. Then review who else serves them, use free public data such as Census Business Builder, and talk with a small number of customers about their past choices. Tie the findings to a specific decision.

What is a landscape review?

A landscape review is a structured look at the other organizations and options serving the same people you serve. It shows where needs are well served, where gaps exist, and which organizations could be partners.

What is the difference between a buyer, a user, and a funder?

The buyer pays or signs, the user experiences the service, and the funder or referrer supports it or sends people to you. In many small businesses and nonprofits these are different people with different goals.

What free market research tools are available?

The U.S. Census Bureau offers Census Business Builder and other small business data tools. The SBA provides a market research guide and free counseling through Small Business Development Centers and SCORE.
