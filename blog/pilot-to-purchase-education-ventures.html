From Pilot to Purchase: Five Questions I’m Taking to EdTech Week

This week I am in New York for EdTech Week. Here are the questions I am bringing for small education teams working to turn a pilot schools like into a purchase someone signs, and a checklist you can use whether or not you are there.

By Gina Wilt, EdD, MBA · October 13, 2026

In short

  • EdTech Week runs October 13 to 15 at Teachers College, Columbia University, and puts founders, educators, investors, and policymakers in the same rooms.
  • I am listening for what helps a small education venture move from a pilot that teachers like to a purchase that someone signs.
  • Five questions sit at the center: who decides, what they will measure, what evidence is enough for now, when the budget is set, and what keeps teachers using the product.

Many education founders I talk with can tell you which classrooms love their product. Teachers ask for it again, students stay with it, and the pilot feedback is warm. Fewer can tell you who will sign the purchase, what that person needs to see first, and when next year’s money gets decided. Those answers often decide whether a good pilot turns into a contract.

Why I am spending the week at EdTech Week

EdTech Week, organized by StartEd, brings the people who build learning products together with the people who teach with them, buy them, and fund them. This year it is at Teachers College, Columbia University.

I have spent more than twenty years building learning programs and education ventures, from the special education classroom to curriculum leadership. I also directed a market-facing professional learning business inside a large public university, building about $585,000 in partnerships and sales with school systems and national education organizations. Today, StrideForward helps small education teams build the evidence, pilots, and partnerships that schools and funders ask for.

A week of conversations in one place is one of the best ways I know to hear what buyers are asking for now and where founders are getting stuck. I plan to listen more than I talk. These are the questions I will be asking.

Five questions I am bringing

1. Who decides, and who pays?

Teachers and students use the product. Principals and curriculum leaders recommend it. A district office, or sometimes a grant or a funder, pays for it. Each of those people needs a different reason to say yes. A pilot that wins over teachers but never reaches the person who signs can end with good feedback and no order.

2. What will the buyer measure?

Founders often measure what their product tracks well: logins, minutes, lessons completed. A curriculum leader may need to show a superintendent or school board something else, such as growth on the district’s own assessment or time teachers get back. Ask early what the buyer will report, and build the pilot to answer that question.

3. What evidence is enough for now?

Under the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA), the U.S. Department of Education describes four tiers of evidence. The entry tier, “demonstrates a rationale,” calls for a logic model informed by research and an effort to study the product’s effects. An early-stage product can meet that tier before it has a formal study, and a clear logic model gives a reviewer something concrete to look at.

4. When is the budget set?

Pilots tend to follow the school calendar, and budgets follow their own. An EdWeek Market Brief report on Digital Promise’s Ed-Tech Pilot Framework noted that many districts lay out next year’s budget by January, so pilot results can arrive too late to count. Find out when the budget is drafted and when the board votes, then plan the pilot so results are in hand before those dates. One of the framework’s eight steps is the district deciding whether to make a full purchase, a useful reminder to plan for that decision from the start.

5. What keeps teachers using it in month three?

After the purchase comes the renewal, and renewals depend on whether teachers keep using the product once launch week is over. From a school’s side, onboarding, short professional learning, and coaching follow-through are part of what they are buying. I want to hear how teams plan for month three as well as launch day.

An example from my own work

In Hawaiʻi, I spent nine months as business architect for HIKI Education. The team had strong, culturally grounded programs, but principals and district leaders had no clear way to understand or contract the professional learning. The work started with the buyer: a school-buyer strategy, packages a principal could read quickly, and a path from a first scoping call to a proposal. That was one organization, and it was professional learning rather than a software product, so I hold it as one example. It is a big part of why my first question to a founder is who signs, and what they need to see first.

What an education venture engagement looks like

When StrideForward works with a small education team on the path from pilot to purchase, the work usually includes:

  • A buyer map: who uses the product, who recommends it, who pays, and who might fund the first year.
  • A pilot designed backward from the purchase decision: the measure the buyer cares about, the right sites, and a timeline that reports results before the budget is set.
  • A buyer-ready packet: the evidence summary, pilot results, pricing, implementation plan, and your privacy and accessibility documents in one place. (We organize them; we do not give legal or compliance advice.)
  • Outreach and follow-up to put it in front of the right people, as a project or with a fractional partner who carries the pipeline with you week to week.

Signs your pilot may not lead to a purchase

  • You know which teachers love the product but not who signs the purchase order.
  • The pilot measures what your product tracks, and no one has asked what the buyer reports.
  • Results are due after the district drafts next year’s budget.
  • A reviewer asked why it should work, and the answer lives in a slide deck instead of a logic model.
  • Nobody owns teacher onboarding after launch week.

If two or more of those sound familiar, start with the buyer map before you plan the next pilot.

Apply it

Try this before your next pilot

  • Write down the name or role of the person who will sign the purchase.
  • Ask that person, or someone close to them, what they will need to report and to whom.
  • Put the budget drafting date and the board vote on your pilot calendar.
  • Pick one or two measures the buyer already uses.
  • Draft a one-page logic model: what teachers and students do, what changes, and the research behind it.
  • Name who supports teachers from week two through month three.

Common questions

Do I need to be at EdTech Week to use this?

No. The five questions work for any pilot, whether you are planning one for next semester or wrapping one up now. The conference is where I am asking them this week.

What level of evidence do schools expect from an early-stage product?

It depends on the funding source. The Department of Education notes that school improvement funds under ESSA require evidence from the top three tiers, while other programs can also use the fourth, “demonstrates a rationale.” A research-based logic model and a plan to study your product’s effects is a sound place to start; stronger evidence usually needs a formal study with an outside research partner.

How long should a school pilot run?

Long enough to see a change in the measure your buyer cares about, and short enough to report before the budget is set. Work backward from the budget date, then check that the pilot window gives teachers enough time to use the product in a normal week.

Does StrideForward only work with education companies?

No. StrideForward works with entrepreneurs, small businesses, and small nonprofits and community organizations as well as education ventures. The same approach applies anywhere a pilot has to lead to a decision someone else makes.
